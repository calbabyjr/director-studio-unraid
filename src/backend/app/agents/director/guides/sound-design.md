# Sound design

## Two layers

- **Diegetic:** sound that exists in the scene. Voices, footsteps, breath, room tone, rain, a radio playing, rope creak, chains, fabric.
- **Non-diegetic:** sound only the audience hears. Score, narration, stylized stingers.

Keep them clearly separated in prompts: the scene's world goes in the soundscape; music goes in the music section.

## Build the soundscape

- **Room tone / ambience first:** every space has a sound (hum of a cellar, distant traffic, wind, water drip). Silence on screen is a room tone, not nothing.
- **Foley for touch:** hands on skin, fabric shifting, footsteps on the specific floor surface, objects handled. Close, detailed foley creates intimacy.
- **Spot effects** only for events the audience must notice.

## Perspective

Sound should match the camera's distance: close-ups hear breath and small sounds; wide shots hear the space and softer detail. Reverb tells the size and hardness of the room.

## Silence and contrast

Dropping ambience to near-silence before a key moment makes that moment land. A sudden sound after silence is more powerful than constant loudness. Let quiet scenes stay quiet.

## Music

- Use score to support emotion the image cannot carry alone, not to explain it.
- Enter and exit on motivated moments (a decision, a cut to a new scene), not mid-beat without reason.
- Match instrumentation to the world: sparse and textural for tension, melodic for release.
- Often the strongest choice is no music.

## Continuity

Ambience and room tone must stay consistent across shots of one scene; a change in ambience signals a change in place or time. Keep recurring sounds (a clock, a heartbeat, a drip) as motifs.

## Writing it into prompts

Describe the room tone, the key foley sources, sound perspective, and whether music is present, e.g. "damp stone-room ambience with a slow water drip, close breath and rope creak, no music". Never repeat dialogue lines in the soundscape or music sections.
