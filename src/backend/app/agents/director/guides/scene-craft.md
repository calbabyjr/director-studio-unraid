# Scene and story craft

## Every scene turns

A scene exists because something changes. Before writing or planning it, name:

- **Goal:** what the point-of-view character wants in this scene.
- **Obstacle:** who or what resists.
- **Turn:** how the situation, power, or feeling is different at the end.

If nothing turns, cut the scene or merge it.

## Beats

Break a scene into beats: each beat is one action and reaction exchange that shifts the balance. Beats map naturally to shots or small groups of shots. Escalate across beats; do not repeat the same beat at the same intensity.

## Shape of the whole

- **Setup:** the world, the people, what is at stake.
- **Escalation:** pressure increases; choices get harder; stakes rise.
- **Peak:** the decisive moment the film has been building to.
- **Release:** consequence, aftermath, a new equilibrium.

Short films need a clear, simple shape more than complexity. One strong escalation line beats several weak ones.

## Character through action

Show character by what people do under pressure, especially small choices and hesitations. Behaviour beats explanation. Give each character a want and something they avoid.

## Dialogue

- Subtext over statement: people rarely say exactly what they mean.
- Short lines, interrupted thoughts, and silences play better on screen than speeches.
- Every line should do work: reveal character, raise tension, or move the plot.
- Let reactions carry weight; a look can replace a line.

## Setup and payoff

Plant objects, lines, or behaviours early that pay off later. Everything the audience is shown with emphasis should matter.

## Point of view and information

Decide whose experience the audience shares. Control information: suspense comes from the audience knowing more than a character; surprise from knowing less.

## Writing it into the project

When drafting or revising script material, keep scene headings, action, and dialogue concrete and filmable. When planning shots from the script, anchor each shot to the beat it serves.
